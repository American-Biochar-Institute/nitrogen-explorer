Nitrogen in biochar: how much it holds, how much a crop can use, and what it does to leaching

Biochar contains nitrogen - from a few pounds per ton in wood biochar to more than 100 pounds per ton in some manure and biosolids biochars. Most is tightly bound and breaks down very slowly, so only a small, variable share becomes available to crops in the first year. Biochar can also affect how much fertilizer or manure nitrogen moves below the root zone, usually reducing losses in sandy soils.

This explorer holds the nitrogen values the American Biochar Institute extracted from its reference library, with the feedstock, the production temperature, the method behind every number and the study it came from. Filters recompute every statistic. Each figure says what its data will and will not support. It supports USDA-NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 336, Soil Carbon Amendment, by providing evidence for feedstock-specific values in place of the single default for plant-available nitrogen in the standard's calculator.

This page shows how much nitrogen different types of biochar contain, how much may become available to crops in the first year, and how biochar can affect nitrogen leaching. Readers can compare the evidence, explore how the values change with different filters, and see the production credits and conservation adjustments ABI proposes for nutrient planning.

Total nitrogen is not available nitrogen

Biochar production processes drive off part of a feedstock's nitrogen as gas. What stays behind is increasingly locked into ring structures in the char (chemists call it heterocyclic or aromatic nitrogen), which soil microbes break down slowly. The forms a crop can take up right away, ammonium and nitrate, are usually a small fraction of the total.

So the total nitrogen on a biochar lab report overstates what a crop can use in the year of application. Established: the direction, and that less of the feedstock's nitrogen survives as production temperature rises. Still uncertain: how much of the locked-up nitrogen becomes available over several years, and at what rate. Few studies follow a biochar past one season (Part 2). Start by selecting one feedstock class, because classes differ severalfold.

What the calculator assumes, and how studies measure availability

The 336 calculator credits 10% of a biochar's total nitrogen as plant-available in year 1. It is the same number the calculator uses for compost and other amendments. The studies below are the evidence a biochar value can rest on. They measure availability four ways, and the four do not measure the same thing:

  • 15N tracer. The biochar is made from plants grown with a heavy-isotope nitrogen label (15N), so its own nitrogen can be followed into the crop and the soil. This is the most direct measure, but it exists only for a few lab-made crop-residue chars.
  • Plant recovery. Extra crop nitrogen uptake with biochar, minus the same soil without biochar, divided by the biochar nitrogen applied. It captures the net effect on the crop, including soil nitrogen the char ties up or frees, so it can be negative.
  • Soil incubation. Extra mineral nitrogen (ammonium plus nitrate) in soil kept moist without plants, compared with the same soil without biochar, divided by the biochar nitrogen added. Same net-effect caveat; values below zero mean net immobilization, where microbes feeding on the char's carbon take up soil nitrogen.
  • Release test. The char alone in sand or water, washed or incubated. It measures what the char can release on its own, not crop uptake, and is off by default.

Established: for wood and crop-residue biochars, year-1 availability is well below 10% and often below zero. Emerging: low-temperature manure biochars, especially poultry and swine manure made at or below 400 °C, can release 10% to 50% in the first months. Thin: biosolids, digestate and food-waste biochars each rest on one or two studies.

What the data support in place of 10%

ABI's Quick Guide sets two book values for each biochar type. The production value is the fertilizer credit, set low on purpose to avoid over-crediting an untested biochar. The conservation adjustment reruns the budget high to stress-test water-quality risk; it is a provisional screening scenario, not a maximum or a validated nutrient-loss limit. In the 336 calculator, the first belongs in the fertilizer value and the second in any check of nutrient rates or N risk. Here both come straight from the year-1 data, one rule each: production = 25th percentile, floored at zero and rounded down; conservation = 90th percentile, rounded to the nearest whole percent. Biosolids has two values from one study, too few for percentiles, so it keeps 0% and 18%. ABI's Quick Guide, Table 2, uses these values. Why the result varies

Biochar can hold ammonium on its surfaces, hold more water in the root zone so less drains through, and tie up nitrogen in microbes when the char carries easily decomposed carbon. It can also do the opposite: open large pores that speed drainage, raise soil pH in ways that speed the conversion of ammonium to nitrate, or simply be overwhelmed when far more nitrogen is applied than a crop takes up.

Established: the effect is variable, and reductions are reported more often in coarse (sandy) soils than in fine (clayey) ones. Still uncertain: how large and how lasting the effect is in the field, especially in fine-textured soils and tile-drained cropland, where few studies exist. Most of the evidence comes from short laboratory column studies.
